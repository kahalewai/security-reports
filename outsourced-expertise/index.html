Incident study·ARTEX agentic campaign·Sept–Oct 2026

Outsourced Expertise  

In September 2026, a not particularly technically sophisticated operator borrowed an open-source AI agent, rented models and disposable servers, and breached seven South Korean financial institutions within days. Why is this significant? This is the AI-generation script kiddie. The risk profile is shifting.

01 How attacks are changing

Nothing here was new except who supplied the expertise.

The weaknesses were ordinary: side systems outside the main login, lookups that trusted a bare ID, limits keyed to IP addresses. Finding and using them used to take an expert. This time the expertise was outsourced to tooling, rented models and disposable servers.

None of the confirmed intrusions required a new class of vulnerability. The entry points were internet-reachable services built for loan brokers, employees and partners, and every technique in the chain predates generative AI.[1,11] What changed is who could do the work.

Then · the script kiddie

Borrowed an exploit

Security teams remember script kiddies, or “skids”: attackers with little skill who got hold of someone else’s exploit and fired it at everything they could reach. The exploit was borrowed, but reconnaissance, judgment and follow-through still had to come from the person, and that limited how far they got.

Now · the new script kiddie

Borrows the expert

The borrowed piece is no longer a single script. It is the full stack an experienced attacker would bring: an agent that maps targets and chooses attack paths, models that reason, infrastructure that rotates on demand, even advice on where to sell the data.[25,1] CrowdStrike’s Adam Meyers described this operator as not particularly technically sophisticated, and the attacks still worked.[23]

The tool

ARTEX, an open-source multi-agent penetration-testing framework released publicly in July and used without modification.[1,14]

The models

DeepSeek v4.1-flash as the main backend through a likely API reseller, with GLM-5.3 and Grok 4.6 in additional Claude Code sessions.[1]

The infrastructure

Rented servers and rotating proxy addresses, replaced as fast as defenders blocked them.[1,8]

The research

Even questions about where to sell stolen Korean data went to an AI assistant.[1]

MITRE describes the pattern directly: with a public model, an adversary is “essentially outsourcing or automating certain tasks to the tool.”MITRE ATT&CK T1588.007 · Obtain Capabilities: Artificial Intelligence [5]

Why the risk is higher now. When expertise can be borrowed, the number of people able to run this level of attack is no longer limited by how many experts exist. Anthropic’s threat intelligence team reached the same conclusion from its own investigations: sophisticated attacks no longer require sophisticated attackers, and the capabilities it documented should be assumed available to anyone motivated to use them.[4] CrowdStrike expects adversaries to keep adding AI tooling to raise their tempo and capability.[1]

Design decisions are becoming the defense that lasts.

Many of the controls that failed here were never strong. They worked because few people had the skill to get past them: a portal no one could find, an IP block that took effort to dodge, a lookup no one bothered to enumerate. Those controls quietly depended on skilled attackers being rare.

A lock

Takes an expert an hour to pick. It protects you only while experts are rare. Against borrowed expertise, it is just a delay.

A wall with no door

Nothing to pick, at any skill level. It protects you from the expert and the script kiddie alike.

The two banks that held were the wall. Investigators say their systems had been built without the flaw the attacker was hunting:[8] a design decision, deliberate or not, that removed the path instead of slowing it down. No detection race, no blocking contest. As expertise becomes something anyone can rent, design decisions like these are becoming the most durable layer of defense an enterprise has.

Withstand the AI aimed at you

Design exposed systems as if every attacker has expert help: complete inventory, server-side authorization, identity-keyed limits, behaviour-based detection. Grounded in this incident’s evidence.

Govern the AI you run

Your own AI keys, agents and testing tools are expertise too. Left ungoverned, they can be stolen and lent to someone else. Grounded in provider and ecosystem evidence.[4,27]

03 The sequence

Sixty-three days from public release to a breached bank.

ARTEX went public on July 26; the first bank attack began September 27. Its latest version shipped three days before that. Discovery came from shared indicators; attribution came from the attacker’s own exposed AI logs.

Before the attack
Dec 2025 – Aug 2026Confirmed

Autonomous attack scaffolding goes public

A model provider documents offensive agent frameworks anyone can download, and a market for stolen AI keys and sessions.

Anthropic’s threat report describes public frameworks reproducing much of the scaffolding for autonomous intrusion, and a criminal supply chain that farms AI API keys and session tokens for loot, compute and cover. It assesses that sophistication is no longer a reliable signal of who is attacking. The report predates and does not mention ARTEX.[4]

2026-07-26Confirmed

ARTEX is released openly

An LLM-driven, multi-agent penetration-testing system is published on GitHub under AGPL-3.0.

The tool connects to external models (DeepSeek, OpenAI and Anthropic among them). A planner agent directs worker agents that run commands and handle HTTP traffic.[9,15,25] Its project page limited use to learning and local verification, not testing of live online systems. That was a notice, not a control enforced in the software.[12]

2026-09-03Reported

First place among ~150 teams

ARTEX wins overall first place at a Baidu-hosted offense-and-defense competition.

Korean reporting notes the competition result as evidence of the tool’s capability ahead of the campaign. A DeepSeek-powered web search feature was reportedly added on September 13.[9,15]

2026-09-24Reported

Latest public version ships

Three days later, the first bank attack begins.

The gap between a capability’s release and its use in the field was measured in days, not review cycles.[9]

Initial compromise
Before Sept 27Confirmed

A two-server setup, assembled from rented parts

A Hong Kong control node plus a VPS hosting ARTEX, with models reached through a likely reseller.

The control node held Claude Code session histories, ARTEX configuration files and agent memory files. DeepSeek v4.1-flash was the primary backend; GLM-5.3 and Grok 4.6 supported additional Claude Code sessions.[1]

Sept 27 – Oct 1Confirmed

Five days of concentrated activity

The attack on KB Kookmin begins September 27; activity across institutions concentrates Sunday through Thursday.

Leaks were reported at Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin Bank, Hana Bank, BNK Busan Bank, Yegaram Savings Bank, Hyundai Capital and Welcome Savings Bank. Woori Bank and NH NongHyup Bank were targeted and not breached.[8,9,10]

Sept 27 – 30Reported · method disputed

Entry through the side doors

A loan-broker inquiry service, an employee mobile work-support system and a staff sales-support system: never the customer banking channels.

At one bank an attacker bypassed identity verification in a mobile inquiry service used by loan agents.[11] Korean reporting describes random values fed into the service to find valid customer numbers before linked records were pulled.[9] An independent analysis points to insufficient server-side validation in legacy systems.[24] Sources describe the mechanism differently, and no step-by-step forensic account has been published.[14]

During the campaignReported

Rotation, not takeover

Data was extracted by querying. Blocked addresses were simply replaced.

Investigators found no system takeover; the attacker got in and extracted information through queries. Two to three attacker IPs overlapped at each bank, and blocking one led to another.[8] In separate sessions, the operator asked an AI assistant where Korean breach data is sold and how to find Telegram data-sales groups.[1]

Discovery
Sept 29 – 30Reported

Detection, hours behind

The first affected institution detects the anomaly more than 15 hours after first access; reported dwell before blocking ran ~30–43 hours.

Dwell figures come from a single outlet and are treated as reported, not confirmed.[18] Banks blocked the attacker’s IPs and suspended affected services on detection.[11]

Oct 1 – 3Reported

Shared indicators surface hidden breaches

Attacker IPs from the first investigation are shared sector-wide; other institutions find their own breaches in their logs.

Collective sharing, not individual telemetry, revealed most of the wave. On October 2, a Korean security firm reported the ARTEX “autonomous penetration test console” title on an attack server. On October 3, the Financial Security Institute said logs at Shinhan Bank showed ARTEX use, identified by a traffic signature.[8,9]

2026-10-07Confirmed

The attacker’s own AI logs tell the story

Exposed open directories reveal session histories, configurations and memory files.

CrowdStrike reconstructed the infrastructure and model stack from directories the operator left browsable. It assesses, with moderate confidence, a Chinese-speaking, financially motivated actor not tied to a named group.[1] The AI tooling that accelerated the campaign also recorded it.

Disclosure & response
Oct 1 – 4Confirmed

Disclosures and an emergency posture

About 25,700 customers at one bank, then 119, 89 and 11 at others; seven institutions confirmed by October 4.

Exposed data at the largest bank included names, phone numbers, annual income and calculated loan limits, plus 66 resident registration numbers and 97 identity-verification identifiers.[8,11] The FSC ordered checks of externally accessible systems, explicitly including AI systems, along with stronger authentication, tighter access control and faster sharing.[11,26] KISA activated a 24-hour emergency posture and sent inspection advisories to about 28,000 companies.[10]

2026-10-08Confirmed

The tool goes closed-source

The developer halts releases, citing misuse, and says the tool was built for learning and authorized testing.

Closing the source stops new releases but cannot recall copies cloned during the months the code was public.[13,15] A Korean-language fork reportedly appeared the same day; it is unverified.[14]

Oct 15 – 19Confirmed

Japan: the same tool, on different servers

GMO Cybersecurity by Ierae confirms ARTEX on one attack infrastructure that targeted about 20 Japanese companies.

Nine of those targets were in the same industry. GMO found six further AI-using attack infrastructures focused on Japan, spanning retail, education, insurance and finance, e-commerce, media and logistics. Eight targeted companies had already published leak notices.[2] In an earlier advisory, GMO described attackers switching IPs just under WAF thresholds across hundreds of addresses.[3]

Ongoing
NowConfirmed

Copies persist; the lesson is in the design

Attribution is unresolved, a broader 16-firm probe continues, and leaked income data carries long-tail phishing risk.

Regulators warned consumers about phishing and loan scams using the leaked data.[13] GMO is investigating whether countries beyond Japan and South Korea are targeted.[2] The tool remains in circulation regardless of its source status.[14]

Two months from release to abuse. Nothing in this sequence is unique to ARTEX. Public offensive agent frameworks already reproduce much of the same attack scaffolding for anyone who downloads them,[4] and a fork reportedly appeared the same day the original closed.[14] The next capability will arrive the same way, and nothing stops someone else from repeating this path with it. Plan for the next 63 days, not the last ones.

05 The most defended sector

Banks carry the heaviest security mandates. Agentic capability reached them anyway.

ARTEX sat in public for 63 days before its first bank attack, and seven institutions confirmed breaches within the week that followed. If the most regulated sector was reachable this fast, treat it as the floor for everyone else.

What the sector already had

Korean financial institutions operate under requirements most enterprises never face.

Network separation

Since 2014, rules under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act required internal business networks to be physically isolated from the internet. Regulators began relaxing them in 2026 for AI security tooling.[39,8]

A sector security institute

The Financial Security Institute traced the attack through bank logs and shared attacker IPs across the sector within days.[8]

Supervisors who moved fast

The FSC and FSS convened emergency meetings and ordered checks of externally accessible systems, AI systems included.[11,26]

AI-assisted testing

Major banks were already building AI penetration-testing and detection programs before the campaign.[36]

Regulators elsewhere already mandate the exact controls that broke down here. New York’s Part 500 requires multi-factor authentication for any user accessing any information system, plus a maintained inventory of information systems, both in force since November 1, 2025.[41] The EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act, applicable since January 17, 2025, requires ICT risk management and threat-led penetration testing for banks and other financial entities.[40]

Mandates set a baseline for the systems an institution can see. Investigators said the breached systems sat across so many management points that institutions had difficulty identifying where the weaknesses were.[8] What separated the banks that held was not a mandate but a design outcome: their systems were built without the flaw.[8] Compliance sets the floor. Design decided the outcome.

06 Where the risk lived

The breach lived in side doors no inventory remembered.

Five API weaknesses explain the entry. Three agentic risks explain how AI, including your own, becomes attack infrastructure.

07 What already existed

Every control that would have helped already existed.

What made them hard was coverage and design assumptions, not knowledge. Each was built for a world where skilled attackers were rare.

01CIS 1 & 2 · CSF ID.AM-02 · API9

A live inventory of every externally reachable app

Puts partner and employee services on the security team’s map, the precondition for every other control.

Grounding
CIS Controls v8.1, Controls 1 and 2; NIST CSF 2.0 ID.AM-02; OWASP API9:2023.[32,35,6]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1595 and T1190 by bringing the broker inquiry service, employee apps and sales-support system into scope.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Coverage gap. Institutions had difficulty identifying where the weaknesses sat.[8] External scanning often can’t determine software versions and avoids intrusive tests, constraints attackers don’t share.[3]
Design note
Systems exposed for operational convenience accumulate outside the view of the team that hardened the flagship channels. A team can only defend what it knows exists.
02CSF PR.AA-03 · CIS 6 · API2

Consistent server-side authentication on every exposed service

Removes the identity-verification bypass that opened the broker service.

Grounding
NIST CSF 2.0 PR.AA-03; CIS Control 6; OWASP API2:2023.[35,32,6]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1190 and close API2.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Partially implemented. Customer channels used authentication beyond IDs and passwords; peripheral systems didn’t.[9,11]
Design note
Investment followed perceived risk, so the strongest controls went to customer channels. Automated tools rank targets the opposite way, by where controls are thinnest.
03OWASP API1

Object-level authorization on every record request

Makes enumerated customer numbers useless: nothing returns unless the requester owns it.

Grounding
OWASP API1:2023 prevention guidance; practitioner guidance on server-side identity, role and ownership checks.[6,28]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1119 and close API1.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Reported as not implemented in legacy systems.[24,9]
Design note
Lookup services built for a small, trusted audience often treat “authenticated” as “authorized”. The assumption held only until someone tested it at machine speed.
04NIST 800-63B-4 · OAT-011 · API6

Rate limits keyed to identity and behaviour, not IP

Caps lookups per session, account or behavioural fingerprint, turning tens of thousands of records into a handful.

Grounding
NIST SP 800-63B-4 throttling; OWASP Automated Threats OAT-011 Scraping, OAT-014 Vulnerability Scanning, OAT-018 Footprinting; OWASP API6:2023.[33,34,6]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1119 and blunt T1090 evasion.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Bypassed. Per-IP blocking was routed around by rotation, in Korea and, separately, in Japan.[8,3]
Design note
IP-centric controls were designed for a time when rotating infrastructure took real attacker effort. Disposable proxies and agentic tooling removed that effort, and the design assumption expired quietly.
05OWASP API3 · CIS 3 · CSF ID.AM-07

Data minimization on lookup responses

A status service returns status, so each compromised query yields far less.

Grounding
OWASP API3:2023; CIS Control 3; NIST CSF 2.0 ID.AM-07.[6,32,35]
What it would have done
Shrink the impact of T1119 and close API3.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Not implemented on the affected flow, which exposed income, loan limits and national identifiers.[11]
Design note
Returning the full record is the fastest way to build a helpful screen. Minimization is a design decision that rarely gets revisited after launch.
06CIS 16 & 18

Testing the whole external surface, with adversary-like automation

Finds what the attacker’s agent finds, first.

Grounding
CIS Controls 16 and 18; CSA’s recommendation to run agentic tools in an organization’s own assessments.[32,14]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1595 and T1190; close API1, API2 and API9.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Coverage gap (inferred). Major Korean banks were building AI-assisted testing before the incident; the breached systems were the less-scrutinized peripheral ones.[36,8] No source links any bank’s testing program to its outcome.
Design note
Testing programs scope to known, prioritized assets, so they inherit every blind spot in the inventory.
07CSF ID.RA-02

Sector threat sharing, including near-misses

Early warning from the first institution probed reaches the rest before their dwell windows close.

Grounding
NIST CSF 2.0 ID.RA-02; Korea’s existing C-TAS sharing platform.[35,10]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1090 and T1595 at sector level.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Partially implemented. Sharing worked after the first breach. But attempts that cause no damage carry no reporting obligation, so the defences that held generated no early signal.[8]
Design note
Sharing frameworks are designed around incidents. The most useful signal, “we were probed and held”, has no path.
08CSF DE.CM · CIS 8 & 13

Behavioural detection on business applications

Flags systematic, evenly paced enumeration in minutes rather than hours.

Grounding
NIST CSF 2.0 DE.CM; CIS Controls 8 and 13.[35,32]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1119.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Coverage gap on peripheral systems; reported dwell of ~30–43 hours.[18,14]
Design note
Monitoring typically sits on systems that move money. A lookup service that only reads data looks low-risk until someone reads all of it.
09CIS 5 · CSF PR.AA-01 · ASI03

AI keys and sessions governed as production credentials

Denies attackers the loot, compute and cover that stolen AI credentials provide.

Grounding
Provider guidance to treat AI keys and agent integrations like production credentials and buy access only through authorized channels; CIS Control 5; NIST CSF PR.AA-01; OWASP ASI03.[4,32,35,30]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1588.007 at the supply end.
Why it wasn’t effective here
An ecosystem-wide gap rather than one reported at these victims. Keys leak through apps, repositories and containers; infostealers lift authenticated sessions.[4,27]
Design note
AI keys were adopted as developer conveniences before anyone classed them as high-value credentials. Secret management hasn’t caught up with how fast AI integrations multiply.
10OWASP ASI02 & ASI04 · CSF ID.RA-09 · CIS 15

Governance of agentic tools and model-access supply chains

Keeps testing agents scope-bound and model access off unvetted intermediaries.

Grounding
OWASP Agentic Top 10 “least agency”; NIST CSF ID.AM-04 and ID.RA-09; CIS Control 15.[30,35,32]
What it would have done
Close ASI02 and ASI04; make enforced scope the expected norm for agentic testing tools.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Not implemented in the ecosystem: the tool’s usage restriction was a notice, model access ran through a likely reseller, and spoofed AI clients are an established harvesting channel.[12,1,4]
Design note
Dual-use testing tools have long run on an honor system that worked while using them took real skill. Agentic packaging removed the skill barrier the honor system silently relied on.
11Provider safeguards · MITRE M1056

Model-provider abuse detection and enforcement

Disrupts model access before or during a campaign, but no single provider sees the whole operation.

Grounding
Provider account enforcement and intelligence sharing; MITRE notes T1588.007 is outside enterprise mitigation.[4,5]
What it would have done
Disrupt T1588.007 and T1682.
Why it wasn’t effective here
Coverage gap across providers: work was split across several model providers and a reseller layer.[1] No provider-side action for this campaign appears in the public record.[12]
Design note
Fragmented visibility works in the attacker’s favour. Enterprises can’t hand this layer to a vendor and consider it covered.

08 Four horizons

Design as if every attacker has expert help. Govern the AI you run.

Twenty actions across four horizons, from the next 48 hours to the next year. Withstand marks defenses against AI aimed at you; Govern marks controls on the AI you run.

Final The takeaway

Script kiddies once borrowed an exploit; the ARTEX campaign shows they can now borrow the expert, as a not particularly technically sophisticated operator turned an open-source agent, rented models and disposable servers into seven breached financial institutions, starting 63 days after the tool went public.

Every technique was familiar, and the controls that failed were the ones that quietly assumed skilled attackers were rare.

The banks that held were protected by design, not detection, so make design decisions your primary control: inventory every exposed system, authorize every request server-side, and govern your own AI so it cannot become someone else’s borrowed expertise.
