The premise

Anything latent in public data should now be treated as found.

Three parties recovered the keys independently, and two of them then handed the job to an AI agent and got the answer from a single prompt. When looking costs nothing, the only defense that runs ahead of the adversary is the one made before the artifact existed.

The only defense that runs before the adversary is the one built in.

On April 30, 2025, a certificate authority ran a key ceremony that produced, as a side effect of a rehearsal step, a second key object for each of eight intermediate CAs. The second objects carried the same labels as the first, and they were the ones that got certified, cross-signed, disclosed and trusted. Their private values were integers below 256. For 535 days anyone holding the public certificate could compute the private key in milliseconds 211.

Nobody did, as far as anyone knows, until a researcher ran a weak-key check across a public corpus of CA certificates on a Saturday afternoon and found the break twenty-seven minutes later 2. Mozilla then recovered the keys without being told how 2. Scott Helme recovered all eight on a laptop in under a second, and reported that an AI agent did the same from one prompt 1113. A Mozilla engineer reported the same result from local agents 2. eMudhra used AI-facilitated analysis to confirm its other issuers were unaffected 2, and Chrome ran a known-weakness tool across its entire trust boundary within an hour of learning the method 2.

One distinction matters and this report keeps it. Whether AI found this flaw is not established: the researcher's own account mentions new tooling run against a public corpus, and eMudhra has suggested AI-assisted techniques may have been involved 251214. What is established, by three parties, is that reproducing the discovery now costs one sentence. That is the fact that changes the risk model. A latent weakness in a public artifact used to be protected by the scarcity of people who would think to look. That scarcity is gone.

In that world, every capability an organization adds after an artifact exists — scanning, revocation, detection, response — is a race that begins after the adversary has started, and the browsers that won this race in a hundred minutes did so because a control was designed into their trust decisions a decade earlier 34. Everything else is still catching up 7. The head start belongs to design, and in this incident three design decisions decided everything.

Decision oneHow a key's identity is verified across an import boundary. Here it was verified by label.
Decision twoWhich anchors a client trusts and how it learns to stop. Browsers had an answer; almost nothing else did.
Decision threeWhether an anomaly can be closed before it is explained. In June 2025 it was.

How it unfolded

The flaw announced itself sixteen months before anyone heard it.

A key ceremony in April 2025 left two keys behind for each CA. One of them failed a CRL signature six weeks later. The investigation resolved the symptom and closed.

The latent chain

Four of six steps were finished before any victim was touched.

No adversary is documented. Every stage was demonstrated anyway, by a researcher, by Mozilla, by an AI agent, and by the third parties who logged 107,107 proof-of-concept certificates in thirty-six hours.

Mapped to MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise 30. The chain below is the path the flaw created; each stage's confidence reflects how directly the sources show it, and stages that were feasible but not demonstrated say so.

Reconnaissance

T1596.003 — Search Open Technical Databases: Digital Certificates

The entire input to the compromise was public. The attack surface was the certificate data itself.

Detail

What happened here

The eight CA certificates sat in CCADB and adjacent public data from May 2025. The researcher's discovery came from running a weak-key check across a corpus of CCADB-disclosed Mozilla and Chrome chains; the run flagged the EC keys within 27 minutes of starting 214. The same corpus is what any adversary would start from. The incident's central lesson lives at this stage: the public certificate data was the attack surface, and the only thing that changed on Oct 17 was that someone looked at it with a test the ecosystem's standard linters did not have 216.

Evidence

Bugzilla 2083449 comments 2 and 50; Help Net Security; paranoid_crypto.

Confidence

High.

Resource development

T1588.004 — Obtain Capabilities: Digital Certificates

No purchase, no theft. The CA private keys were derived from their public keys in milliseconds.

Detail

What happened here

ATT&CK describes this technique as buying or stealing certificates. Here no purchase or theft was needed: each P-384 private scalar was an integer below 256, so recovery is at most 255 scalar multiplications 211. Three independent parties did it: the researcher (proof by signed message), Mozilla at 21:48 UTC Oct 17 with no knowledge of the method, and Helme on Oct 20. A Mozilla engineer and Helme each report that an AI agent reproduced the recovery from a single prompt 21113. The acquired capability is not a leaf certificate but the issuing authority itself: six TLS intermediates trusted by all four major root programs and, under sub-technique T1588.003, two code-signing intermediates trusted by Microsoft 23.

Evidence

Bugzilla 2083449 comments 0, 2, 5; Helme; Google; CyberScoop.

Confidence

High.

Resource development

T1587.003 — Develop Capabilities: Digital Certificates

With an intermediate's key, anyone can mint certificates for any domain without touching the CA. 107,107 were minted in 36 hours.

Detail

What happened here

Between Oct 20 09:40 and Oct 21 22:02 UTC, 107,107 certificates signed with six of the eight keys were logged to CT by third parties, including 106,050 proof-of-concept certificates naming google.com and precertificates for emudhra.salesforce.com; every signature verifies under the corresponding CA key 2. Helme separately forged a certificate for api.stripe.com 11. Such certificates carry whatever revocation pointers the forger chooses — in this case, almost none — so a client that checks revocation only for the end-entity certificate finds nothing to check 2.

Evidence

Bugzilla 2083449 comment 44; Helme.

Confidence

High.

Credential access · collection

T1557 — Adversary-in-the-Middle

A forged certificate plus a network position yields transparent TLS interception against any client that still trusts the chain and does not enforce CT.

Detail

What happened here

Helme demonstrated the client side: with default settings, curl reported the forged chain as verified, Python completed the handshake, and Node reported the connection authorized. On Windows, CryptoAPI accepted the chain unless revocation checking was enabled, and even then only one of the two chain routes returned as revoked 11. Mozilla, Google, CERT-In and Unit 42 all describe interception, impersonation and traffic modification as the primary consequence 3491. No source reports a real-world interception; whether one occurred against non-CT clients during the 535-day window is unknowable from public data 7.

Evidence

Helme; Google; Mozilla; CERT-In; Unit 42; Jackson.

Confidence

High for feasibility, which was demonstrated. No evidence of execution.

Defense evasion

T1553.002 — Subvert Trust Controls: Code Signing

Two of the eight keys belong to code-signing CAs trusted by Windows. Signed malware would present as software from a real publisher.

Detail

What happened here

EM OV CS CA - G3B and G3C are code-signing intermediates trusted by Microsoft 2. An adversary holding those keys can issue a code-signing certificate and sign malware so that it presents as software from a real publisher to any verifier that does not check revocation 119. Apple's frameworks do not trust these CAs; Windows does 8. The same cross-signing that extended the TLS keys into AAA Certificate Services also cross-signed both code-signing CAs 2. No source reports signed malware in the wild.

Evidence

Bugzilla 2083449 comments 0 and 2; Apple; CERT-In; Helme.

Confidence

Medium: feasibility established from trust relationships; not demonstrated by a test in the sources.

Initial access

T1195.002 — Supply Chain Compromise: Software Supply Chain

The highest-leverage targets are installers, self-updaters and CI runners that fetch code over HTTPS with no independent signature check.

Detail

What happened here

These clients trust an on-disk CA bundle, check neither revocation nor CT, and so remained vulnerable until each bundle was rebuilt 11. The Go team treated its fix as an urgent ecosystem-integrity release and hard-coded the eight keys into the standard library's verifier 12; the community patch tracker lists well over sixty runtimes, distributions and tools that shipped updates, with several still pending in November 7. Unit 42's guidance to inspect build pipelines and bundled trust anchors reflects the same concern 1.

Evidence

Helme; The Hacker News; Jackson; Unit 42.

Confidence

Medium: mechanism argued by multiple researchers and reflected in vendor response; no compromise observed.

Where the weakness lived

The cryptography was sound. The design around it was not.

Six OWASP Top 10:2025 categories apply and they chain: an insecure design produced a cryptographic failure, silently; the signals it produced were not followed; and the integrity and supply-chain layers made containment slow.

The 2025 edition 29 is the right one for this incident: its new A03 and A10 categories describe two of the mechanisms here more precisely than any earlier edition could. CWE entries 31 give the concrete weakness beneath each category. Ordered by causal position, not list number.

Primary driver

A06:2025 — Insecure Design

The ceremony was designed so that the error was easy to make and impossible to see.

Detail

Why it applies

The category covers risks that originate in how a system was conceived rather than how it was coded. Four design choices combined: keys destined for export were generated and then wrapped, unwrapped and re-imported as a rehearsal inside a production ceremony; the resulting key objects carried identical labels; the step that bound a public key to a CA certificate was manual and label-driven; and the control gate for that step was human maker-checker review with no cryptographic check that the public key being certified corresponded to the key the ceremony had intended to generate 2. Each is a reasonable choice in isolation. Together they left the correctness of a globally trusted CA resting on a person distinguishing two identically named handles.

Specific weakness in this incident

Two same-label key objects per CA from the day of the ceremony; CSRs built from the re-imported objects; signed by the root and sent to Sectigo; no reconciliation that the HSM now held roughly twice the expected number of CA keys 2. The June 2025 CRL discrepancy — the one moment the design's error surfaced — was resolved by switching to whichever key verified, which is the same design decision repeated 102.

Citations

Primary driver

A04:2025 — Cryptographic Failures

A P-384 key must be a random 384-bit integer. These were integers below 256.

Detail

Why it applies

This is the failure as it manifested. A P-384 private key must be a uniformly random integer in a space of roughly 2³⁸⁴; the eight published keys were integers below 256, carrying three to eight bits of effective entropy 211. A certificate built on such a key is structurally perfect — valid curve point, valid encoding, valid signatures — and cryptographically empty. The algorithm was sound; the key material was not 9.

Specific weakness in this incident

Eight CA private scalars recoverable by enumerating at most 255 candidates, certified by eMudhra's root, cross-certified by Sectigo, disclosed to CCADB and included in browser trust lists for 535 days.

A03:2025 — Software Supply Chain Failures

The trust-store supply chain is static artifacts on independent release cadences with no revocation channel.

Detail

Why it applies

The 2025 category expands beyond vulnerable components to the whole process of building, distributing and updating software. Mozilla's root list is vendored into certifi, Node, Go, Java, Debian, RHEL, Electron, Docker base images and dozens of tools, each on its own release cadence and none with a revocation channel 7. What existed

Fourteen controls were available. Six would have stopped it on day one.

None of them is exotic. The ones at the source are design properties of a ceremony; the ones at the edge are capabilities added later, and even the best of those protect only browsers.

Would have stopped it · Apr 30, 2025

1 · Cryptographic verification of key identity across every import boundary

Sign a test vector with each re-imported object and verify against the key captured at generation; compare key hashes before and after.

Detail

Grounding

NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 Rev. 5 22 (assurance of public-key validity and private-key possession); FIPS 186-5 23 (key-pair validation); CA/B Forum BR §6.1.1.1 18 (prepare and follow a key generation script). FIPS 140-validated modules perform a pairwise consistency test at generation — but a wrap/unwrap/re-import is not a generation event, so that built-in test never fired on the objects that were certified.

What it would have done

Disrupted the chain at its first link. A test signature per re-imported object, or a simple comparison of key hashes before and after the rehearsal, would have shown that the two objects did not match before any CSR was built.

Why it was not effective here

Not implemented. The ceremony script treated the rehearsal as a procedural step rather than a cryptographic one; correctness of the subsequent CSR step was delegated to key labels and human review 2.

What made this hard

The certified control — a FIPS 140-2 Level 3 HSM — did exactly what it is certified to do: generate strong keys and report success on every operation. The gap was conceptual. The team understood the HSM as the guarantor of key quality, and the rehearsal was approved precisely because it looked like a logistics exercise rather than a key-generation event 5. Most PKI operators would have drawn the boundary in the same place.

Would have stopped it · Apr–May 2025

2 · Weak-key screening before certification, cross-certification and disclosure

The tool existed since 2022. Chrome ran it across its whole trust boundary in an hour once it knew to.

Detail

Grounding

Google's paranoid_crypto 16, open-sourced August 2022 specifically to detect weak artifacts produced by black boxes such as proprietary HSMs, including EC public-key checks; the research lineage behind it; community linters badkeys, zlint and pkimetal 2.

What it would have done

Caught the eight keys at any of four moments — at the ceremony, before Sectigo cross-signed on May 14, 2025, at CCADB disclosure that month, or during any root-program monitoring pass in the following seventeen months.

Why it was not effective here

Coverage gap. The ecosystem's standard linters performed algorithmic checks for RSA and blocklist checks for ECDSA but had not implemented the baby-step giant-step small-scalar test that paranoid_crypto includes. Google's internal paranoid_crypto pipeline scanned certificates logged to CT, and these intermediates had never issued anything, so they were never in scope. The repository itself was archived in August 2026 2.

What made this hard

A decade of weak-key research had been operationalized for RSA; the EC analog had been published but not absorbed into the tools everyone actually runs. Nobody owned the question of whether anyone had ever scanned a dormant CA key for this — not because anyone declined to, but because every scanner was scoped to what it was built to watch.

Would have stopped it · Apr 30 or Jun 2025

3 · Key-inventory reconciliation at ceremony close-out

The plan called for 24 key pairs. The HSM held roughly twice that for two of the three series.

Detail

Grounding

BR §6.1.1.1 18 (ceremony report by a qualified auditor); NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 22 (lifecycle accounting); CERT-In's post-incident recommendation of independent verification of key identifiers and CSR mappings 9.

What it would have done

A count of key objects against the script's expected output would have flagged the anomaly the same day, and again during the June 2025 investigation.

Why it was not effective here

Not implemented. A community question on the bug asked directly whether there was any accounting for twice the number of keys; it is unanswered 2.

What made this hard

Organizations inventory certificates, which are visible, far more often than key handles, which live inside partitions and are easy to create and hard to see. A ceremony that creates objects as a side effect of a rehearsal breaks the assumption that object count equals plan.

Would have surfaced it · 2025

4 · Witnessed ceremony with a published record of every deviation

Required by the Baseline Requirements when a sub-CA will be certified by a root the CA does not operate — which Sectigo's cross-signing triggered.

Detail

Grounding

BR §6.1.1.1 18: the CA shall follow a key generation script, have a qualified auditor witness or record the ceremony, and have the auditor issue a report opining that the script was followed.

What it would have done

A witness report documenting the approved wrap/unwrap deviation and the resulting object inventory would have made the two-key condition visible to the auditor in 2025, to the WebTrust engagement, and to the root programs on request.

Why it was not effective here

Partially implemented and unverifiable from public record. eMudhra published a ceremony report for its February 2024 root ceremony; the equivalent for April 30, 2025 — script, witness report or video, HSM logs — has been requested repeatedly and not produced as of Nov 2. Whether an auditor observed the deviation is an open question 2.

What made this hard

Auditors attest that a procedure was followed, not that the keys it produced have the right mathematical properties. A deviation approved in advance is, by definition, procedurally compliant. The control was never designed to catch this, and it is not a criticism of the auditor that it did not.

Would have caught it · Jun 2025

5 · Anomaly-depth root-cause discipline

The June 2025 CRL failure was the weak keys announcing themselves. Asking why a second key verified would have led straight to them.

Detail

Grounding

CCADB Incident Report guidelines 21, which require a root-cause analysis with contributing factors and action items that address causes rather than symptoms.

What it would have done

The CRLs had been signed with the original keys and failed to verify against the certified ones 2. Asking why a second key verified where the first did not would have led to the two-object condition and, with one lint, to the weak scalars — sixteen months before public discovery.

Why it was not effective here

Partially implemented. A 5-Whys analysis was performed, but it stopped at incorrect CA key configuration. The fix that made the CRLs verify — switching to the other key — closed the ticket and simultaneously normalized the weak keys as the operational ones. The report did not record that two key pairs existed 102.

What made this hard

Incident response has a gravity toward the first explanation that makes the symptom disappear, especially under a 24-hour response clock and when no end-entity certificates are at stake. The anomaly did not look like a security event; it looked like a mapping error. This is the hardest control on the list to get right because it depends on curiosity surviving urgency.

Would have distinguished the keys

6 · Hardware attestation that a certified key was generated in-module and never imported

Turns trusting the operator's description of the ceremony into verifying the device's description of the key.

Detail

Grounding

Vendor HSM attestation features — at least one major HSM vendor supports manufacturer-signed key-origin attestation, per a community contributor on the bug; Chrome Root Program's stated intent to pursue remote key attestation requirements 2.

What it would have done

Distinguished the originally generated objects from the re-imported ones by their provenance, independent of labels. A root program or cross-signer requiring attestation would have received proof that the certified keys were not the ones the HSM generated.

Why it was not effective here

Not required by the Baseline Requirements; not used.

What made this hard

Attestation is a design primitive, not a process, which is exactly why it is robust to the kind of approved deviation that defeated the procedural controls here — and also why it has to be chosen at procurement time rather than added later.

At the ecosystem · detect before trust is extended
Would have caught it · May 2025

7 · Cryptographic linting as a gate on cross-certification and CCADB disclosure

The gates existed and linted — for structure, policy and known RSA weaknesses. Eight structurally perfect certificates passed every one.

Detail

Grounding

Chrome Root Program Policy 20 and Mozilla Root Store Policy 19 (continuous monitoring and linting obligations); CCADB 21; the now-filed proposal CCADB-Tools#185 to make weak-key screening a prerequisite for disclosure 2.

What it would have done

Stopped the keys at the two moments trust was extended beyond eMudhra: Sectigo's cross-signing on May 14, 2025, and CCADB disclosure the same month.

Why it was not effective here

The EC small-scalar check was not among the tests. Chrome's own account of the gap is the fair one: in its words, “a defense-in-depth ecosystem should have caught a flaw of this nature automatically” 2.

What made this hard

The ecosystem had built impressive machinery for catching malformed certificates and had, reasonably, assumed that HSM-generated EC keys were the one thing it did not need to test.

Worked, within its scope

8 · Certificate Transparency enforcement

Browsers were protected before anyone knew there was a problem. Nothing else was.

Detail

Grounding

RFC 6962 26; Chrome, Apple and Firefox CT enforcement 7; ATT&CK M1054 30.

What it did

Because no certificate chaining to the six TLS intermediates was ever logged before Oct 17, 2026, no forged certificate could have been accepted by a CT-enforcing client 3.

Why it was not more effective

Scope. CT enforcement is a browser property; curl, Python, Node, Go, Java, git, package managers and embedded devices do not enforce it. An attacker targeting those clients would simply not log the forged certificate, and the CT-based weak-key pipeline would never see the intermediate either 112.

What made this hard

CT is the clearest success in this incident and the clearest illustration of the thesis: a control designed into the browser's trust decision worked without anyone acting; the same control bolted on after the fact to the non-browser world does not exist.

At the relying party · limit blast radius and contain
Worked for browsers

9 · Pushed revocation for intermediates

OneCRL, CRLSet, CRLite, disallowed CTL, Apple's config updates — a block in 100 minutes with no user action. For everything else: sixty releases and counting.

Detail

Grounding

Mozilla OneCRL (2015) and CRLite; Chrome CRLSets; Microsoft automatic disallowed-CTL updates; Apple's automatic certificate configuration 3487.

What it did

Chrome had a key-hash block live 100 minutes after the report; Firefox completed OneCRL distribution in under three hours; blocking by public key meant the cross-signs were covered automatically.

Why it was not more effective

No equivalent exists for the non-browser ecosystem. Containment there required more than sixty separate software releases, container rebuilds, hard-coded blocklists and manual edits to macOS's bundle file, and remains incomplete 712.

What made this hard

Mozilla shipped pushed revocation over a decade ago; for the rest of the ecosystem there has been almost no investment in the problem. That is a collective design debt, not any single organization's omission.

Undermined four ways

10 · Strict revocation checking at egress, proxies and gateways

Soft-fail defaults, end-entity-only checks, six-month CRL caches, and endpoints that refused common user-agents.

Detail

Grounding

BR §4.9 and §4.9.7 18; RFC 5280 25; Unit 42 interim guidance 1.

What it would have done

After the Oct 18 revocation, any client that checked the full chain and refused to proceed on failure would have rejected forged certificates presenting the revoked intermediates.

Why it was not effective here

Clients soft-fail by default. Many check only the end-entity certificate, and forged leaves carry no revocation pointer. eMudhra's root CRLs carried next-update dates roughly six months out, so a compliant client could cache the pre-revocation CRL for months. And for a period, eMudhra's CRL endpoints returned 403 to common command-line user-agents 211.

What made this hard

Revocation checking is unreliable by design because availability was prioritized over security when the protocols were built. Turning it on is not a configuration change; it is a decision to accept outages. Few organizations have made that decision consciously, which is why so few have made it at all.

Would have made it irrelevant

11 · Curated, minimal trust stores for machine-to-machine clients

A webhook client that trusts twelve CAs instead of the whole public list was never exposed.

Detail

Grounding

RFC 5280 §6 25 (trust anchors are chosen by the relying party); Mozilla's trust-store operator guidance 15; Go's decision to enforce a key blocklist in its own verifier, demonstrating per-application trust policy is practical 12.

What it would have done

Internal service-to-service traffic, CI runners and SDK calls that trust only the handful of CAs they need would never have trusted an eMudhra chain in the first place.

Why it was not effective here

Most machine clients inherit the full public root list from a language runtime or OS bundle because that is the default and curating it is work with no visible payoff until a day like Oct 17 117.

What made this hard

The public trust store is designed for a browser that must reach any site on the internet. A payment webhook client does not need that property, but it gets it for free and nobody takes it away.

Would have neutralized the supply-chain path

12 · Artifact signature verification independent of transport

A forged TLS certificate cannot forge a package signature.

Detail

Grounding

ATT&CK M1045 Code Signing 30; signed packages and checksums in modern ecosystems.

What it would have done

Installers and updaters that verify artifact signatures would have been unaffected even over an intercepted connection.

Why it was not effective here

Widely absent. Curl-pipe-shell installers, self-updaters and many CI fetch paths use TLS as their only integrity control 1112.

What made this hard

TLS was never intended to be a code-integrity mechanism; it became one by convenience. Treating transport authentication as artifact authentication is a design shortcut almost everyone has taken.

Would have shrunk the migration

13 · CA agility — ACME Renewal Information and short-lived certificates

~30,800 certificates had to move before Oct 27. Roughly 97% were ACME-issued, and ARI was not in use.

Detail

Grounding

RFC 9773 28; Mozilla Root Store Policy §6.1.3 19 (proactive subscriber communication and mass-revocation planning); the industry's scheduled move to 47-day lifetimes 1.

What it would have done

With ARI, the CA could have signalled early renewal and clients would have rotated automatically onto a new chain.

Why it was not effective here

ARI was not in use by eMudhra or its subscribers; the affected ecosystem's certificates ran about 200 days; eMudhra asked the root programs for an extended transition window, which Mozilla policy does not permit 25.

What made this hard

Issuance pipelines are built for renewal, not replacement. The difference only shows when the CA itself is the thing that must change.

Detects only what the forger logs

14 · CT monitoring for one's own domains

Valuable against ordinary misissuance; weak against a key compromise, because an attacker targeting non-CT clients has no reason to log anything.

Detail

Grounding

RFC 6962 26; public monitors; Unit 42 and CERT-In guidance 19.

What it would have done

Detected the proof-of-concept forgeries that third parties logged for google.com and emudhra.salesforce.com 2.

Why it was not effective here

Detection depends on the attacker logging the certificate.

What made this hard

It belongs in the program; it should not be mistaken for coverage of this scenario.

The pattern across all fourteen. The six controls at the CA are design properties of a ceremony and of an operator's relationship with its HSM; four would have stopped the incident on the day it began, and two more within six weeks. The two ecosystem controls did their jobs within their scope. The six relying-party controls are capabilities added after the fact, and even the ones that worked brilliantly for browsers do not exist for the clients where the real exposure lives.

The takeaway

The head start

Capable AI has collapsed the distance between a latent weakness and a found one: anything derivable from a public artifact should now be assumed derived. Every capability added afterward — scanning, revocation, detection, response — begins its race after the adversary has started, and this incident shows that race being lost by the entire non-browser internet. The only defense that runs before the adversary is the one built in. That is the head start, and the designs that earn it are small and specific: how a key is verified across a boundary, which anchors a client trusts, whether an anomaly can be closed before it is explained.
